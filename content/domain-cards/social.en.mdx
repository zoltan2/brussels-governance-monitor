## Samusocial: more than four in five families turned away in July (11 August 2026)

On **Tuesday 11 August 2026**, **Samusocial** released its July figures for emergency family accommodation. Of the **299 families** that requested a place, **244 were refused**, more than four in five. Refusals rose **83.5%** compared with June.

Those refused families included **238 children**, of whom **65 were under two** and **15 were infants under six months**. The operator notes that **one in four** people it houses is a child, and reports **1,555 people** on the emergency accommodation waiting list kept by **BelRefugees**.

### What these figures actually measure

The data cover the month **before** the announced closures. The federal government cut **300 places** at the end of June, then **700 places** on **16 July 2026**, under the Brussels Deal, with closures due **before winter** (see the next section). July therefore measures how saturated the system was **before** that capacity disappeared, not after.

Samusocial reports a "strong and continuous increase" in requests, particularly from families, at a point where its facilities can already no longer absorb them.

**Institutional note**: Samusocial is a non-profit of the **COCOM/GGC**, the Brussels bicommunity authority responsible for assistance to persons. The reception places being cut, by contrast, are **federal** (Fedasil). A refusal at Samusocial therefore does not redirect anyone to a federal desk: it redirects them to the street, to a municipal **CPAS/OCMW**, or to the voluntary sector. That is the mechanism by which a federal decision becomes a regional and municipal burden.

## Reception: five organisations call a demonstration against the cut of 1,000 places (18 August 2026)

On **Friday 7 August 2026**, five organisations called for a mobilisation on **Tuesday 18 August 2026 at 5.30 pm, place de l'Albertine**, against the removal of **1,000 reception places** for applicants for international protection in Brussels: the **BelRefugees Citizens' Platform**, the **Bico Federation**, the **Samusocial**, the **Ama Federation** (reception houses and homelessness services) and the **joint trade union front**.

### What is being cut

The scheme concerned is the **"Brussels Deal"**, set up in **2022**: **42 million euros a year** funding **2,000 reception places** in the Brussels Region, created to absorb the saturation of the network of **Fedasil**, the federal reception agency.

The cut came in two stages: **300 places** announced in late June 2026, then a further **700 places** on **16 July 2026**. In total, capacity falls from about **2,000 to about 1,000 places**. The federal minister for Asylum and Migration defends the measure by pointing to the fall in asylum applications and to the scheme's character as a **temporary emergency measure**.

### Why this concerns the Region and the municipalities

The Brussels Deal is a **federal** decision, but its effects land on **regional and municipal** competences: emergency accommodation, social assistance from the public social welfare centres and management of public space. According to the associations, about **1,000 people already live on the street** in Brussels, and the closure could double that number.

The most notable institutional signal is the presence of the **Samusocial** among the signatories: this is not a campaigning nonprofit but the **bicommunity operator (COCOM)** running the Region's main emergency accommodation infrastructure. A Brussels public operator is therefore publicly calling to demonstrate against a federal decision.

Schaerbeek's acting mayor had already voiced fears of a worsening situation in the North Quarter on 17 July.

**Institutional reminder**: Fedasil is a **federal** agency. The Samusocial is a nonprofit of the **COCOM**, the Brussels bicommunity authority competent for assistance to persons. The **public social welfare centres** are municipal, funded by the municipalities and the Region.

## Unemployment reform: impact on Brussels CPAS

### What is this about?

Since 1 January 2026, unemployment benefits in Belgium are limited to a maximum of two years. People exceeding this threshold lose their entitlement. The measure is applied in three waves:

- **1 January 2026**: people with more than 20 years of inactivity
- **1 March 2026**: people with 8 to 20 years of inactivity
- **1 April 2026**: people with 2 to 8 years of inactivity

In total, **185,700 people** will lose their benefits between January 2026 and July 2027 in Belgium. In the Brussels-Capital Region, approximately **42,000 people** are affected (Actiris estimate, cumulative Jan. 2026 – Jul. 2027).

### What happens to excluded people?

The federal government budgeted the reform on the assumption that one third of those excluded would find work, one third would turn to the CPAS (social integration income) and one third would have sufficient resources (partner, savings). Several sources contradict this projection:

- **National Bank of Belgium**: return to work would concern **10 to 20%** of those excluded, not 33%
- **Vivalis** (study based on CBSS data, March 2025): of the 24,700 excluded persons under 55, **21% would find work**, **32% would turn to the CPAS**, **42% would end up with no identifiable income** and 5% would move to sickness-disability insurance
- **Court of Auditors** (February 2026): the government's assumption is described as "not very realistic"; the share moving to the CPAS is "probably underestimated"
- **2015 precedent** (limitation of integration allowances): 72% of those excluded had not found work after 6 months

### Brussels particularly exposed

Brussels accounts for around 10% of the Belgian population but would concentrate one third of social integration income recipients and one third of people without income resulting from the reform (Vivalis). The number of social integration income recipients in Brussels already stood at **46,911** as an annual average in 2024 (IBSA), up 12% since 2020. The most affected municipalities are Saint-Josse-ten-Noode, Molenbeek-Saint-Jean, Saint-Gilles and the City of Brussels, where close to 4% of the population is affected.

### Diverging data: why the numbers do not match

Initial official data from the FPS Social Integration indicate that 20 to 27.5% of those excluded in January turned to the CPAS. However, CPAS on the ground report presentation rates at the counter of 40 to 50%, and even higher in Brussels. The gap is explained by the nature of the data: FPS figures count administratively registered files (payment or encoding data), while CPAS count people who present themselves at the counter, including cases under review or pending. The delay between application and registration is several weeks.

Update (23 June 2026): before the Chamber's Social Affairs committee, the federal minister for employment quantified, across the first three waves and based on provisional statistics from the FPS Social Integration, actual CPAS take-up at 28.1% in Brussels (30.4% nationally, 22.5% in Flanders, 36% in Wallonia). This measurement consolidates the lower bound of the initial data; the gap with counter presentation rates is still explained by the registration delay and differences in counting scope.

### The blind spot: non-take-up

Without systematic cross-referencing of databases (ONEM, ONSS, health insurers, CPAS) via the Crossroads Bank for Social Security (CBSS), it is impossible to determine what happens to people who do not turn to the CPAS: employment, undeclared work, financial dependence on a relative, leaving the country or non-take-up of rights. Non-take-up of social rights in Brussels is a structural phenomenon documented by Vivalis as early as 2016 in a thematic report on social under-protection in the Brussels Region. The 2025 Vivalis study is the only one to have cross-referenced CBSS data to project the trajectories of excluded Brussels residents; it estimates that 42% of those excluded under 55 would end up with no identifiable income.

### Budgetary impact

The federal government has allocated EUR 300 million in compensation for CPAS in 2026. According to the Court of Auditors, this would cover needs for 2026 but would prove insufficient for subsequent years, with expenses estimated at EUR 709 million by 2029. The cumulative cost for all Belgian CPAS is estimated between EUR 631 million and over one billion over the legislative term.

Sources: ONEM; FPS Social Integration; Vivalis (March 2025); Court of Auditors (Feb. 2026); UVCW; IBSA; RTBF (March 2026).

## Vivalis Social Barometer 2025 (15 April 2026)

On **15 April 2026**, the **Observatory for Health and Social Affairs** (Vivalis's research department) publishes the **Social Barometer 2025** — the reference report on the state of poverty and social health inequalities in the Brussels Region.

**Monetary poverty**: **23%** of the Brussels population is at risk of poverty, compared with **7% in Flanders** and **13% in Wallonia**. The apparent drop versus 2023 (28%) remains within the statistical margin of error.

**Social Integration Income (RIS)**: **47,304 recipients on 1 January 2025** — more than in Flanders (**45,616**) despite a population five times smaller; Wallonia counts **77,207**. Among 18-64-year-olds, the share of recipients has risen from **3% in 2002 to nearly 7% in 2025**. In **Molenbeek** and **Saint-Josse**, **more than one adult in ten** receives the RIS.

**Geography of poverty**: **six of the ten municipalities with the lowest incomes in Belgium** are located in Brussels — **Saint-Josse, Molenbeek, Anderlecht, Koekelberg, Schaerbeek and the City of Brussels**. Within the Region itself, poverty rates vary two- to threefold between municipalities.

**Children**: **1 Brussels child in 3** lives in poverty.

**Housing** — the most deteriorated indicator:
- **55,572 households** on the waiting list for social housing (**+78% in 15 years**), waits often exceeding 10 years
- **24%** of Brussels residents live in substandard housing
- **30%** in overcrowded housing (including **50% of households with children**)
- The **poorest 2%** devote over **50% of their income to rent**, leaving them roughly **€10 per person per day** to live on

**Highly vulnerable populations**:
- **~50,000 undocumented persons** (≈ **4% of the population**)
- **9,777 homeless or precariously housed persons** (Bruss'Help count, Nov. 2024), **up 25% in two years**

**Health inequalities**:
- **Life expectancy** gap of around **5 years** between the wealthiest and poorest municipalities
- **Diabetes** three times more prevalent among the poorest 20%
- **1 Brussels resident in 5** shows symptoms of anxiety or depression

Sources: [RTBF — Precariousness rises in Brussels, social barometer indicators (15 April 2026)](https://www.rtbf.be/article/la-precarite-augmente-a-bruxelles-selon-plusieurs-indicateurs-du-barometre-social-11710138); [Vivalis — Observatory for Health and Social Affairs](https://www.vivalis.brussels/fr/informer-et-communiquer/etudes/etudes-de-lobservatoire). ## Water poverty: 23.2% of Brussels households, against 8.5% in Flanders (27 August 2026)

On **Thursday 27 August 2026** the **King Baudouin Foundation** published its water poverty barometer, produced by researchers from the **ULB** and the **Universiteit Antwerpen**. Water poverty here means devoting an excessive share of disposable income, once housing costs are deducted, to the household water bill.

Across Belgium, **one household in eight is affected, or 12.8%**. In the Brussels-Capital Region it is **close to one household in four: 23.2% in 2024**.

### The only region not improving

The gap between regions is the first fact. How it has moved is the second, and the more telling one.

| Region | 2021 | 2024 | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Brussels | 21.9% | **23.2%** | **+1.3 points** |
| Wallonia | 19.3% | 15.6% | −3.7 points |
| Flanders | 9.1% | 8.5% | −0.6 points |

Brussels is **the only one of the three regions where the share is rising**. The gap with Flanders widens from 12.8 to 14.7 points in three years. This is therefore not a national cyclical effect: Wallonia started from a level close to that of Brussels and cut its own by a fifth over the same period.

The barometer identifies the most exposed groups as people at risk of poverty, households with low work intensity, single-parent families, people living alone and people in poor health.

### What this figure measures, and what it does not

It is a **budgetary** indicator, not one of physical access: it measures the weight of the water bill in a household budget, not a disconnection or a missing connection. It should be read alongside the price of water in Brussels, whose trajectory is documented in the dossier on Vivaqua.

## Includo: six million euros contested inside the majority (24 August 2026)

**Includo** is the social service of the **STIB**, created in 2024, which works with homeless people and people with addictions in metro stations. It fields around twenty social workers given nine weeks of training and two fitted-out buses offering hot drinks and shelter, alongside the **SubLINK** referral scheme. Its budget is about **six million euros**.

An MP from **Les Engagés**, a party of the regional majority, is asking for that funding to be shifted to the specialist operators already working in Brussels, chiefly the **Samusocial**, on the grounds that they hold greater expertise and that the STIB should concentrate on service reliability and station safety.

The **Mobility Minister (Groen)** defends the scheme: a purely security-driven approach moves people on without taking them in, and Includo is present on the network around the clock.

The argument exposes a question of remit: should a transport operator run a social service because it is the first place where homelessness becomes visible?
