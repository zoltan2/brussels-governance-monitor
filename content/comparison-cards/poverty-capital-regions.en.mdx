## Context

The at-risk-of-poverty-or-social-exclusion rate (AROPE) is the European Union's reference indicator. It counts people in at least one of three situations: equivalised disposable income below 60% of the national median income, severe material and social deprivation, or very low household work intensity. Eurostat publishes it for each NUTS-2 region through the EU-SILC survey (dataset ilc_peps11n). This level makes it possible to compare the capital regions themselves.

## The data compared

In 2025, **33.6%** of residents of the Brussels-Capital Region were at risk of poverty or social exclusion, one resident in three. That is the highest rate of the 27 NUTS-2 regions that contain the capital of an EU member state. The Brussels rate is higher than that of:

- Vienna (29.8%), by 3.8 points
- Berlin (24.4%), by 9.2 points
- Île-de-France (20.9%), by 12.7 points
- North Holland (16.9%), the Amsterdam region: the Brussels rate is almost double

## On income alone, Vienna and Brussels within the same margin

The at-risk-of-poverty rate (AROP) looks at income only. On that measure, Vienna shows a higher rate than Brussels in 2025: **25.7%** against **23.3%**. The 2.4-point gap stays within the survey's margin of uncertainty: for Brussels, the 95% confidence interval ran from 23.4% to 29.2% in 2024 ([Statbel, via indicators.be](https://indicators.be/fr/i/G10_POV/)). Berlin (18.7%), Île-de-France (16.9%) and North Holland (14.8%) follow.

In Brussels, this rate was 28.8% in 2023, 26.3% in 2024 and 23.3% in 2025; these year-to-year changes should be read with the same margin of uncertainty. Brussels' higher AROPE rate therefore comes from the indicator's two other components: severe material and social deprivation and very low work intensity.

## The paradox confirmed

This comparison reinforces the "Brussels paradox" already visible in GDP data. Of these five regions, Brussels has the highest GDP per inhabitant (73,100 PPS in 2023, against 66,100 in North Holland and 61,500 in Île-de-France) and the highest rate of poverty or social exclusion. Several factors explain this situation:

- **The commuter effect**: wealth produced in Brussels largely benefits non-residents who redistribute across the Flemish and Walloon peripheries.
- **Demographic structure**: Brussels concentrates a young, diverse and partly low-skilled population in a labour market that demands bilingualism.
- **Housing costs**: with an average private rent of EUR 1,346 per month and a homeownership rate of only 40%, housing absorbs a disproportionate share of modest households' income.

## The weight of material deprivation

Beyond the monetary poverty threshold, **38.3% of Brussels residents** report being unable to face an unexpected expense of EUR 1,100, compared with 13.2% in Flanders. This concrete vulnerability translates into difficulties accessing healthcare, housing and quality food.

## Limitations of the comparison

At NUTS-2 level the region matches the capital city for Brussels, Vienna and Berlin. Île-de-France and North Holland cover a wider area than Paris and Amsterdam. The poverty threshold is set against each country's median income: in a richer country the threshold is higher, and a capital region there can show a higher rate at the same standard of living. Regional EU-SILC figures rest on a sample: a gap of a few points between two regions is not a precise ranking.
